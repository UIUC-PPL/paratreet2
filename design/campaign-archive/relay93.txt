2.  WHY IT WORKS -- AND IT IS NOT THE TRADE I EXPECTED
====================================================================

The prediction on record was that capping trades broadcast bytes for walk
fetches, with an optimum where they cross.  The fetch side barely moves:

    arm    cached_nodes    requests      canopy_fills   per process
    unset   131,038,862   12,098,928              0
    128      95,813,837   12,002,873        347,592          339
    1        95,504,825   11,974,523        371,745          363

cached_leaves and cached_particles are unchanged to three digits across every
cap -- the actual DATA fetched never changed.

*** 347,592 CANOPY FILLS ACROSS 1024 PROCESSES IS 339 PER PROCESS.  The
    uncapped run broadcasts 34,835 canopy entries to every process and each
    process turns out to need about 339 of them -- UNDER 1%.  This is not a
    trade between two costs.  It is declining to broadcast a payload that 99%
    of its recipients never read. ***

AND IT EXPLAINS WHY -s 1 IS WORSE DESPITE ONLY 7% MORE FILLS (371,745 against
347,592).  The count barely changes; WHICH levels changes.  The top levels are
wanted by everybody and are served by a handful of canopy chares, so
withholding them converts a broadcast into a fan-in.  That is a latency and
contention effect, not a volume one -- which the 230 ms spread on -s 1 shows
directly.

NOTE ON THE COUNTER, because relay93 got this wrong.  relay93 reported
"requests flat across every cap", measured with requests_served, which counts
CacheManager::serviceRequest.  A canopy miss ABOVE the TreePiece level never
reaches it -- Traverser.h:186 routes those to tc_proxy.requestData, and
TreeCanopy::requestData calls CacheManager::restoreData directly.  That is
exactly the traffic the cap redirects.  canopy_fills (branch commit 3548ac3)
closes the hole; the wall results were never in doubt but the explanation was
unsupported until now.

-s does NOT touch this: the cap applies to send_size in Driver::loadCache,
AFTER every recvTC has arrived and after sortStorage has sorted all of them.
The two fixes are independent and item 2 survives the -s result intact.

====================================================================
4.  PREDICTING THE OPTIMAL -s  (Kale's question, model only -- NOT MEASURED)
====================================================================

    C_ship(s)  = alpha * P * s          alpha = entry_bytes / B, B ~ 16 GB/s
    C_lazy(s)  ~ beta * P / s           fan-in on the shallowest withheld
                                        level: b^d0 chares serving P requesters

Both carry P, so minimising gives  s* = sqrt(beta/alpha)  and **P CANCELS**.

*** THE DISCRIMINATING PREDICTION: if that is right, -s 128 is also optimal at
    16 and 64 nodes.  If instead the optimum moves as 1/P, the lazy term is a
    latency CHAIN (~ L log_b(N/s)) rather than a fan-in and the model is wrong.
    One sweep at 16 and 64 nodes settles it, and it is cheap. ***

ON THE THREE VARIABLES KALE RAISED:
  PROCESS COUNT   model says no dependence; the alternative says strong
                  dependence.  This is the cheapest and most discriminating
                  experiment.
  DATA SIZE       enters only through canopy depth log_b(N_treepieces), which
                  does not appear in the fan-in model at all -- only the
                  shallowest withheld level matters.  So 24B and 80B should
                  keep roughly the same s* while pack_n itself grows.  That is
                  the CONVENIENT outcome and therefore the one to distrust.
  CLUSTERING      the model does NOT capture this and I expect it to be where
                  the real dependence lives.  Non-uniform density unbalances
                  the canopy, so a few chares own most of the boundary and the
                  relevant beta is a TAIL (max_k requests(k) * t_serve), not a
                  mean.  That pushes s* UP: the more clustered the data, the
                  more you want to ship eagerly.  The 80M set is the natural
                  control if its density structure differs.

WHAT WOULD TURN THIS INTO A FORMULA: s* at 16 / 64 / 128 nodes on 2B
(discriminates the two lazy models), plus the same sweep on 80M (separates size
from clustering).  Four small jobs and one large one.  I would not ship a
formula fitted to a single point -- 128 is one optimum, at one scale, on one
dataset, and nothing yet shows it transfers.
