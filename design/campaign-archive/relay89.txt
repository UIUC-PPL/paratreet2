2.  BUT pruned = 0 EXACTLY, AND THAT CHANGES THE READING
====================================================================

Kale asked for three states precisely so a pruning DEFECT could be told from
correct pruning.  The middle category came back empty -- not small, exactly
zero, in both reps and in the canopy split too.  I did not expect that and it
deserves the scrutiny rather than a footnote.

THE ENCODING IS WHAT KALE ASKED FOR.  touch_state is a monotone raise:
0 = never examined, 1 = examined by a certificate that PRUNED, 2 = descended
into or particles read.  So "pruned" survives only for a node that was
examined by at least one pair and descended by NONE.  The category is
recoverable; it is genuinely empty.

*** THE READING THAT FITS: PRUNING HAPPENS BEFORE THE FETCH, NOT AFTER.  A node
    is fetched because a traversal committed to descending into its parent, so
    every node the walk actually ASKS about is one it goes on to use.  A node
    the certificate would reject is rejected at its parent and never requested.
    That is why the middle state is empty.

    WHICH MEANS THE 76.5% IS NOT A PRUNING DEFECT.  IT IS PREFETCH OVERSHOOT.
    relay88 measured 15.5 nodes per addCache reply -- replies ship a subtree,
    not a node.  At 23.5% used that is about 3.6 useful nodes per 15.5 shipped.
    The lever is therefore the BUNDLE (cache share depth -D, currently 3), not
    the certificate. ***

HONEST LIMIT ON THAT CLAIM.  It rests on my instrumentation returning a true
zero rather than missing the state-1 write, and the state-2 writes clearly
work (23.5% is non-zero and stable).  The check that would settle it costs one
run: count the doOpen calls that return FALSE with a cached target, as a raw
counter independent of the per-node state.  If that counter is also ~0,
pruning-before-fetch is confirmed; if it is large, my state-1 write is being
lost and the never/used split still stands but the interpretation does not.
I have NOT run that check.

4.  THE htram REQUEST-SPREAD CHECK -- THE PREMISE IS WRONG, TWICE
====================================================================

From the arm-B trace, inverting BEGIN_PROCESSING (p[5] is the source, the
executing PE is the file) to get each requester's spread of servers:

    DISTINCT DESTINATION PROCESSES per requesting PE (of 128)
      min 1   p10 3   median 6   p90 10   max 17
    share of a PE's requests on its top-k destinations
      top-1  median 52.1%   top-4  median 97.1% (min 59.0%)   top-8  median 100%

*** REQUESTS DO NOT SPREAD OVER 127 PROCESSES.  The median PE talks to SIX, and
    four destinations carry 97% of its traffic.  The walk's spatial locality
    concentrates exactly as Kale's alternative supposed, so the "4-item buffer
    takes ~50 ms to fill" arithmetic does not hold. ***

    per (source PROCESS -> dest PROCESS) channel -- a nodegroup aggregator's
    granularity, since the cache is per process:
      1,795 channels   median 2.32 req/ms  -> a 4-item buffer fills in 1.72 ms
      p90 15.89 req/ms -> 0.25 ms          busiest pair 54.81 req/ms -> 0.07 ms
    per (requesting PE -> dest process): median 0.55 req/ms -> 7 ms to fill

So at process granularity buffering fills in under 2 ms typically; at per-PE
granularity it is 7 ms and still too slow.  If aggregation is built it belongs
on the nodegroup, not per PE.

*** AND THE SECOND HALF OF THE PREMISE IS ALSO WRONG, IN THE OTHER DIRECTION.
    Kale noted the request side is only 253 MB of 8.7 GB so the ceiling is
    small.  That is true of BYTES and false of TIME:

      requestNodes  43.7 ms per PE mean, 18.2% of window wall, max/mean 2.17x
      addCache      33.5 ms per PE mean, 14.0% of window wall
      otherCache     2.3 ms per PE mean,  1.0%

    SERVING REQUESTS COSTS MORE PE TIME THAN PROCESSING THE FILLS -- 43.7
    against 33.5 ms -- at 14.9 us per request served.  Bytes are a bad proxy
    here.  The ceiling is a share of 18.2% of wall, not of 2.9% of bytes. ***

WHAT THAT DOES NOT SAY: most of those 14.9 us is presumably the lookup and
packing of 15.5 nodes, which aggregation does not remove -- it batches the
message, not the per-key work.  I have not separated per-message overhead from
per-key work, so the addressable fraction of the 18.2% is unmeasured.  The
honest summary is that the concentration objection to aggregation is dead, the
bytes objection is the wrong metric, and the real question is now the
per-message overhead share -- which is a different measurement.
